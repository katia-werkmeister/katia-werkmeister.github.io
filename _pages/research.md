# Work in Progress
### Firm Training Standards
with [Cäcilia vom Baur](https://sites.google.com/view/cacilialipowski/)

<details>
<summary style="cursor: pointer;">Abstract</summary>
<p>Firms underinvest in general skills training because trained workers can be poached. We show that precise training standards, specifying concretely which skills firms must teach, mitigate this market failure. In a simple model, precise standards rule out cheap training, making every firm's workers equally well trained. Hence, firms gain little from poaching, and training pays even for firms that could not otherwise retain their trainees. We study German apprenticeship standards, which have become substantially less precise over time. They are also less precise in occupations with more firm-specific skill needs and greater technological uncertainty, suggesting that regulators trade off the costs and benefits of precision. Exploiting quasi-random variation in precision that arises because regulators copy wording across occupations, we find that less precise standards reduce firms' investment per trainee and erode training participation. Consistent with the poaching mechanism, the drop in participation is driven by firms exposed to poaching and occurs shortly after a standard is revised. Changes in trainee selection also play a role.</p>
</details>

### Alumni Networks, First Job Placements, and the College Selectivity Premium
with [Moritz Seebacher](https://moritzseebacher.github.io/), [Cäcilia vom Baur](https://sites.google.com/view/cacilialipowski/), and [Ludger Woessmann](https://sites.google.com/view/woessmann-e)

<details>
<summary style="cursor: pointer;">Abstract</summary>
<p>What is the causal role of college alumni networks in graduates’ first job placement and earnings differences across colleges? Descriptively, graduates from the same college cluster at the same firm three times more than firm and college observables predict. Using resume data from LinkedIn profiles covering over 90 million college×cohort×firm cells, and exploiting plausibly exogenous variation in the timing of alumni firm-to-firm moves around graduates’ job search, we find that each additional alumnus raises the employment probability at this firm by 13%. Alumni in management and those from the same major have a stronger effect. While alumni from elite colleges do not provide stronger ties than alumni from less-selective colleges, they work at better-paying firms. This affects the college selectivity wage premium: alumni networks account for at least 4% of the entry-level wage gap between elite and less-selective colleges.</p>
</details>
